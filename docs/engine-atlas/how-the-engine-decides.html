How the engine decides — and what 10,147 real decisions say about it

A plain-language model of the machinery that turns "what has this player done before" into "what should you do now" — followed by the first honest measurement of whether it works.

Corpus · HandHQ 50NL · Jul 2009 10,147 scored decisions 230 players Online cash — live 1/2 unproven

Part one — the mechanism

The engine answers one question over and over: this villain is about to act — what will they do, and what is that worth to you?

It does that in five steps. Everything else in the codebase is detail hanging off these five.

  1. 1

    Record every action

    Every time a player folds, calls, bets or raises, that decision is stored along with the situation it happened in.

  2. 2

    File it in a drawer

    Each decision goes into a labelled drawer. The label has six parts: which street, how wet the board was, their position, whether they were the aggressor, whether they were in position, and what they were facing. Six parts means a very large number of possible drawers.

  3. 3

    Look up the drawer — and widen if it's thin

    To predict, the engine opens the drawer matching right now. If there are fewer than three hands in it, it drops one part of the label and opens a broader drawer. It keeps widening — six times — until it finds enough evidence, or falls back to "how the average player behaves."

    This step is where the problem is
  4. 4

    Blend with the crowd

    The villain's own numbers are mixed with the population average, weighted by how much you've actually seen. Three hands barely move the needle; three hundred hands mostly override it.

  5. 5

    Turn it into money

    The predicted fold / call / raise percentages feed the EV maths, which prices each of your options and recommends the best one. A wrong prediction in step 3 becomes a wrong recommendation here.

Part two — where the decisions actually land

Step 3 widens the drawer until it finds evidence. So the first honest question is: how often does it get to use a specific drawer, and how often does it end up guessing from the crowd?

Part three — the finding that changed our minds

The obvious reading of that chart is "we need more data so the specific drawers fill up." We tested the opposite idea instead: what if we ignored most of the label and just pooled everything together?

To test it properly, every version scored the exact same 10,147 decisions, so the comparison is like-for-like rather than two separate averages.

Why this wasn't obvious before

The engine's own diagnostics said the opposite, because they compared different decisions rather than different methods.

The drawer chart shows level 1 having much lower error than level 6, which looks like specificity paying off. But a decision only reaches level 1 when there's lots of history on it — and those are the easy ones. It was comparing easy decisions to hard ones and crediting the difference to the method.

The test above avoids that trap by running every version over the identical decision set.

Is it a real difference or just noise?

"Real" means the 95% confidence interval excludes zero. The pooling result is about eleven standard errors from zero — it is not a close call. "Drop texture first" is the one comparison that genuinely lands on noise, which settles a question that had been argued from a code comment for months.

What this means in one sentence. The engine is currently slicing your opponents' histories too finely — three observations in a very specific spot is a worse guide than three hundred observations in a broad one, and right now it prefers the three.

Part four — the second measurable error

Separately: when the engine predicts how often someone folds to a bet, it predicts roughly the same number no matter how big the bet is. Real players do not behave that way.

Why the shape matters more than the size

Notice the error changes direction: on small bets the engine over-estimates folding, on medium and large bets it under-estimates. A single correction applied everywhere cannot fix a sign flip.

That rules out "the base number is a bit low" and points at the curve that maps bet size to fold rate. It is a different repair, and we would have made the wrong one from the average alone.

Part five — where the engine helps you, and where it doesn't

Averages hide this completely. Split by what the villain is facing and how many players are in the pot, and the model has one strong region and one region where it is actively worse than assuming nothing.

Part six — what changed in the engine today

Separate from the measurements above, the decision maths was rewritten to handle multiway pots properly. Previously every formula assumed exactly one opponent, which is the wrong game: your table is nine-handed and a large share of pots are multiway.

The most consequential piece: the rule for whether a bet is a value bet.

Also fixed: defence frequency now divides across the field. Facing a ¾-pot bet, one defender must continue 57% of the time — but each of three defenders only needs 25%, because the bettor only needs everyone to fold. Applying the heads-up number per player made the engine expect far too much defence multiway.

What to do with all this

Three repairs are queued, in the order the evidence ranks them:

  1. Stop over-slicing the histories — the finding in part three. Largest measured effect by a wide margin.
  2. Fix the fold-versus-bet-size curve — part four. Second largest, and it converts directly into money through the bluffing maths.
  3. Work out why the model dies against aggression — part five. Least understood, arguably most important, because it is the situation where you most need help.

The caveat that bounds everything above

Every number on this page comes from one corpus: online cash games, 50NL, July 2009. It measures whether the engine predicts that population. Your live 1/2 game is a different population, and the transfer is an assumption, not a result — nothing here is evidence about your actual game until the app has scored enough of your own recorded hands.

One nuance worth holding: your per-opponent samples at a live table are far smaller than this corpus's. If anything, that makes the over-slicing problem worse for you, not better.
